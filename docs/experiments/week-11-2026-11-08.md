## Proposed use case

A shared DAC writes independent, slowly changing DC biases to two high-impedance plunger gates on a silicon double quantum dot. One gate retains its operating point during an update to its neighbor. Channel count, gate assignment and electrical limits are working hypotheses. The two-channel bench represents each electrode and interconnect with capacitance and a load leakage current. Demonstrating charge-state control would require a device model or hardware and a device-level observable.

## Requirements to resolve with the device owner

Ask which two gates need independent adjustment, their absolute voltage windows and polarities, and the smallest useful change of each gate near its operating point. Ask for gate plus interconnect capacitance, leakage, intended wiring, the location and temperature of the DAC and device, and the interval during which the gates must stay quiet. Ask for the allowable gate disturbance during a neighboring update and the allowed error at the start and end of a readout. If supplied as an energy tolerance, request the gate lever arm so it can be converted to a voltage budget.

The existing specification draft proposes 1 kupdate/s, a 10 µs acquisition screen and 61 µV over a 1 ms hold as engineering targets for exploration. Apply the hold budget to the change from the initial held value. Track DAC step size, absolute target error, switch-opening offset, hold drift and refresh ripple separately.

## First bench to draw

Provisional cell name: tb_gate_bias_hold_2ch. Start at TT, 27 °C, 1 V. Use a low-impedance PWL source as the first write bus. Route it to two existing tg_n65 switches in parallel. Each switch feeds its own node VA or VB with a storage capacitor to VSS and a separate load capacitor and leakage element. Use a pair of complementary controls per TG and one-hot write selects. During a DAC update both TGs are open. Wait for the write bus to settle before closing the selected TG. Run with an ideal bus first, then connect the CDAC through a unity-gain buffer model to expose acquisition, drive and isolation requirements.

Example stimulus: write A at 0.375 V, open A, set the bus to 0.5 V, then write and open B while observing A. These are the nominal code-3 and code-4 levels. Use separately initialized traces for a quiet hold and a neighboring update. Plot Vbus, VA, VB, select_A and select_B. Record acquisition error at the end of track, the voltage step across switch opening, drift over 1 ms and peak disturbance on A during B's update relative to the quiet-hold trace. The first run can use an explicitly assumed 1 pF storage capacitor, 100 fF external load and 1 pA test leakage as a sensitivity probe. Sweep against the ranges in the existing worksheet as time allows.

With constant 1 pA leakage and 1.1 pF combined capacitance, the ideal 1 ms drift magnitude is about 0.91 mV. The corresponding net leakage for 61 µV/1 ms is about 67 fA. These are hand estimates for the stated test parameters. Room-temperature simulations evaluate circuit behavior at that corner. Low-temperature retention and noise require suitable device data and models.

The current floating CDAC top plate has about 0.885 pF of array capacitance. A comparable sampling capacitor can materially shift Vout through charge sharing. A behavioral buffer permits initial interface tests. A physical buffer's input capacitance, output swing, settling and power are later measurements. A second prospective block is an address and refresh controller that stores each channel code and enforces the open–update–settle–sample–hold sequence. Choose the segmented-core experiment after observing disturbance at the gate during its valid interval. The matched eight-code binary/thermometer MOS-reset comparison remains an open Week 11 task.
